Model - predict

Calculate the predicted body weights of penguins on Biscoe, Dream, and Torgersen islands by hand.

\[ \widehat{body~mass} = 4716 - 1003 \times islandDream - 1010 \times islandTorgersen \]

  • Biscoe: \(\widehat{body~mass} = 4716 - 1003 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{0} - 1010 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{0} = 4716\)
  • Dream: \(\widehat{body~mass} = 4716 - 1003 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{1} - 1010 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{0} = 3713\)
  • Torgersen: \(\widehat{body~mass} = 4716 - 1003 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{0} - 1010 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{1} = 3706\)

Models with categorical predictors

  • A categorical predictor is encoded using dummy variables. A predictor with \(k\) levels requires \(k - 1\) dummy variables.

  • The first level of the categorical variable is the baseline level. In a model with one categorical predictor, the intercept is the predicted value of the outcome for the baseline level (when all dummy variables equal 0).

  • Each slope coefficient describes the difference between the predicted value of the outcome for that level of the categorical variable compared to the baseline level.

Changing the baseline level

  • By default, R uses the first level of a categorical variable as the baseline level.

  • We can change the baseline level by reordering the levels of the categorical variable.

bm_island_fl_fit <- linear_reg() |>

tidy(bm_island_fl_fit)
# A tibble: 4 × 5
  term              estimate std.error statistic  p.value
  <chr>                <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 (Intercept)        -4625.     392.      -11.8  4.29e-27
2 islandDream         -262.      55.0      -4.77 2.75e- 6
3 islandTorgersen     -185.      70.3      -2.63 8.84e- 3

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 \\ &- 262 \times \text{islandDream} \\ &- 185 \times \text{islandTorgersen} \\ &+ 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

Model - formula, deconstructed

Biscoe: \(\text{Dream} = \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}}\) and \(\text{Torgersen} = \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 - 262 \times \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}} - 185 \times \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}} + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \\ &= -4625 + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

Dream: \(\text{Dream} = \color{#9E2C6A}{\mathbf{1}}\) and \(\text{Torgersen} = \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 - 262 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{\mathbf{1}} - 185 \times \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}} + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \\ &= -4887 + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

Torgersen: \(\text{Dream} = \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}}\) and \(\text{Torgersen} = \color{#9E2C6A}{\mathbf{1}}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 - 262 \times \color{#5C97CB}{\mathbf{0}} - 185 \times \color{#9E2C6A}{\mathbf{1}} + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \\ &= -4810 + 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 \\ &- 262 \times \text{Dream} \\ &- 185 \times \text{Torgersen} \\ &+ 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

Dream: For Dream penguins, the model predicts body mass to be lower, on average, by 262 grams than for Biscoe penguins, all else held constant (i.e., for penguins with the same flipper length).

Torgersen: For Torgersen penguins, the model predicts body mass to be lower, on average, by 185 grams than for Biscoe penguins, all else held constant (i.e., for penguins with the same flipper length).

Model - interpret, intercept

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\text{body mass}} &= -4625 \\ &- 262 \times \text{Dream} \\ &- 185 \times \text{Torgersen} \\ &+ 44.5 \times \text{flipper length} \end{aligned} \]

Intercept: For Biscoe penguins with a flipper length of 0 mm, the model predicts body mass to be -4625 g. The intercept is not meaningful in context.

What does this say about which model is better for predicting body mass?

Adjusted \(R^2\): Fit and complexity

  • \(R^2\):
    • measures the proportion of variability explained by the model
    • increases as more predictors are added to the model, even if the new predictors are not meaningful
  • Adjusted \(R^2\):
    • measures the proportion of variability explained by the model, with a penalty for the number of predictor coefficients in the model
    • penalizes the model for adding unnecessary predictors, therefore does not automatically increase when another predictor is added
